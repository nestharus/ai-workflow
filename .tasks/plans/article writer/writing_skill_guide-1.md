# Writing Skill: Articles and Posts

## Core Principle

Every sentence has a goal. Every paragraph has a goal. Every technique has a goal. Optimize around those goals to illustrate something as effectively and resoundingly as possible.

## Scope and Limits

This guide is optimized for reframing, exploration, and thinking-in-public.

When the goal is decision-making, instruction, or execution, some constraints (especially around closure) may need to be relaxed deliberately.

If applying this guide starts to feel like compliance rather than thinking, stop. The guide exists to remove failure modes, not to replace judgment.

If you can feel yourself trying to satisfy a rubric, you're probably writing for the rubric, not the idea. That's permission to break rules deliberately.

## The Four Rubrics

Apply these in order. All four must pass before the piece is done. If one fails, fix it and run all four again.

---

### Rubric #1: Story Beats

The piece must hit these beats while hiding that it's hitting them:

1. **Tells a story** - The reader is led through a journey, not presented with conclusions.

2. **Powerful hook** - The opening challenges assumptions or creates tension. Questions often work better than statements.

3. **Opens with a problem** - The problem should be felt, not just stated.

4. **Connects and shows the problem is real** - Ground it in something concrete before abstracting.

5. **Has a hypothesis** - State it with confidence when it arrives.

6. **Tries to prove the hypothesis** - Walk the reader through the logic. Let them arrive with you.

7. **Has a lens shift** - The reader ends with a different way to see the problem. Not "here are three takeaways." What changes in how they look at things.

8. **Uses tempo to drive points** - Short sentences punch. Long sentences explain. Vary deliberately.

9. **Separates ideas into groups** - Each section does one job.

10. **Hides the format** - The skeleton should be invisible. Headers describe content, not rhetorical function. If the header could be "Problem / Solution / Conclusion," rewrite it. "Explicit structure" names a concept. "The tradeoff" names a rhetorical job. Prefer the former.

---

### Intro Structure

The intro has to do several things quickly:

1. **Tension hook** - Challenge an assumption the reader holds
2. **Immediate reframe** - Explain why you said that (don't leave them hanging)
3. **Problem + hypothesis (first pass)** - State the situation and a first attempt at the answer
4. **Acknowledge confusion** - Signal you know they're not sold yet
5. **Concrete hypothesis** - Give the answer again, plainly

**Critical: the hook and reframe must connect directly to the hypothesis.** If your hypothesis is about X, your hook challenges an assumption about X, and your reframe points at X. You keep hammering X. The intro is not setup for the hypothesis. It *is* the hypothesis, approached from multiple angles.

How you execute each move is style. They can compress into two sentences or spread across a paragraph. The acknowledgment can be a question, a "let me be specific," or a shift in register. The concrete hypothesis can be two words or a worked example.

Don't copy an example's shape. Follow the structure with your own moves.

If you can label your own intro beats while reading, it's too visible. Compress or scramble.

### Rubric #2: AI Tells

Eliminate these patterns:

**Emdashes** - Replace with colons, periods, or restructure the sentence.

**Sentence-starting conjunctions** - Never start sentences with But, And, So, Or. Restructure or cut the conjunction. These are AI tells that mimic casual speech without earning it.

**Conjunction substitutions** - "Still," "Yet," "However," "Therefore" at sentence start are just "But/And/So" in disguise. Move them mid-sentence or cut them entirely.

**Hidden triads** - Comma-separated clauses can hide triads. "Do X, do Y, do Z" is a triad even without periods. Break it into two sentences or vary the structure.

**Comma-separated parallel clauses** - This is distinct from hidden triads. Watch for sentences that string together parallel grammatical structures with commas:

- "how X works, how Y flows, how Z propagates"
- "where A happens, where B occurs, where C exists"
- "that does X, that holds Y, that remembers Z"
- "every A, every B, every C"

These read as lists even when the content isn't a list. They signal AI because they impose false symmetry on ideas that don't require it.

The same problem exists when commas are replaced with periods or dashes. "How X works. How Y flows. How Z propagates." is the same pseudo-list with different punctuation. So is "How X works — how Y flows — how Z propagates." Changing punctuation doesn't fix the underlying parallel structure.

Fix by: combining two items with "and" (no comma), expanding one item into its own sentence with different structure, or rewriting to break the parallel construction entirely. Two items joined by "and" is fine. Three or more parallel clauses almost never are.

**Repeated sentence structures** - Three "If" sentences in a row, or "Find X... Find Y... Find Z..." patterns signal AI even when the content requires listing. Vary the structure: use a question, change the verb, flip the order.

**Fluff** - Every word must earn its place. Cut:
- "one of the few ways"
- "something like"
- "it's worth noting that"
- "the question becomes"
- Anything that sounds like throat-clearing

**Cliches** - Kill them unless you're subverting them.

**Negative language** - Negatives are pivots, not defaults. Most humans speak positively most of the time.

Distinguish between:
- **Defensive negation** ("this isn't about X"): usually filler
- **Clarifying contrast** ("this applies when Y dominates Z"): often necessary

Use negatives only to exclude a plausible misreading. Never use them to posture or argue.

**Triads and closure signals** - The problem with triads isn't symmetry. It's that they signal completion. They present a thought as finished, compress uncertainty into rhetoric, perform confidence rather than preserve inquiry.

"More structured. More editable. More durable." This packages the idea. The reader stops thinking and starts consuming.

Avoid forms that signal closure. Triads don't invite thought; they package it. Break the pattern when the thought is still live.

**Hedging** - Don't hedge with vague modal verbs ("could be," "might," "perhaps"). If the claim is conditional, state the condition.

Bad: "This might improve performance."
Good: "This improves performance when latency dominates throughput."

Remove "might/could" but allow "when/if/under." This keeps strength and correctness.

**Parallel structure overuse** - Repeating the same sentence structure multiple times signals AI. Vary it.

**Performative intent** - The most important AI tell is not cadence or symmetry. It's writing that knows it's being read and shapes itself accordingly.

Sentences that technically pass every other check still read as artificial when they're optimized to "land," summarize, or demonstrate insight rather than express a live thought.

If a sentence feels like it's trying to impress, clarify, or conclude, it's probably overworked. Prefer sentences that exist because the thought exists, not because the reader is watching.

**Moralizing and status language** - Covert superiority signals trigger eye-rolls even when the prose is clean.

Kill these:
- "Everyone gets this wrong"
- "People don't realize"
- "The real truth is"
- Dunking on strawmen

Replace with:
- "I used to think X"
- "A thing that surprised me"
- "Here's where I got stuck"

This preserves intensity without claiming authority you haven't earned.

### Rationed Patterns

Some patterns aren't banned but become tells when overused. These must earn their place. Even when they seem earned, find an alternative if you've already used the pattern recently.

**Staccato closers** - Ending paragraphs with two short punchy sentences. "X is true. Y follows." This works once per piece, maybe twice. More than that and it becomes a tic.

**Parallel openings** - "Most X. Most Y." or "Some X. Others Y." or "This works. That doesn't." The contrast structure is useful but becomes rhythmically deadening when repeated.

**Judgment lists as prose** - Sentences that are really ranked items: "X is the strongest. Y is moderate. Z is weakest." If you're ranking, either own the list format or restructure so the ranking isn't the skeleton.

**Imperative chains** - "Do X, then Y, then Z" or "Pull the lever, see what happens, start over." Commands in sequence. One per piece maximum.

**Contrast pairs** - "Not X but Y" or "Less about X, more about Y." Also includes the inverted expectation form: "This should do X. It doesn't." or "X exists. Y doesn't." Any structure where two short units set up and then subvert, affirm and negate, or establish parallel opposites. Useful for reframing. Loses power fast when repeated.

**Rhetorical questions followed by answers** - "What does this mean? It means..." Once per piece at most. Twice and it feels like a formula.

The test: scan the piece for patterns. When usage starts to look spammy, cut instances until it doesn't. There's no hard count—a long piece can sustain more than a short one. Trust your eye.

Then check distribution. Back-to-back instances read as a tic. Evenly spaced instances (every third paragraph, for example) read as a template. Local clusters of different patterns in the same section read as formulaic even if each individual pattern only appears once. The goal is irregular distribution that doesn't call attention to itself. Also check spacing: two instances of the same pattern back-to-back or within a few sentences reads as a tic even if the total count is acceptable. Spread them out or cut one.

### Rubric #3: Flow

Check transitions between every section and paragraph:

**Abruptness** - Does the reader know why we're shifting? If not, add a bridge.

**Bridges** - Questions work well. "What would that look like?" "Which raises a question:" The end of one section should point toward the next.

**Circular structure** - The ending connecting to the beginning creates closure.

**Momentum** - Each paragraph should make the reader want the next one.

Test: Read it aloud. Where do you stumble? Where does your attention drift? Those are flow problems.

### Rubric #4: Adversarial Robustness

A piece can pass the first three rubrics and still fail when it meets a skeptical reader. This rubric tests whether the writing survives contact with an adversarial audience.

**Intent alignment**

Before publishing, write one sentence answering: "If this post succeeds, what will the reader think differently?"

Then write one sentence answering: "What do I not want the reader to conclude?"

These don't go in the post. They discipline the writing.

**Pushback pass**

Identify the strongest objection a knowledgeable skeptic would raise.

Either:
- make that objection impossible, or
- make it irrelevant, or
- explicitly narrow the claim so the objection no longer applies

This keeps confidence while preventing avoidable derailment.

**Worst-case reader test**

Identify the most likely bad-faith or low-context reader. Ask: "What would they accuse this post of saying?"

Decide whether to:
- preempt that reading,
- accept it,
- or sharpen the post so that reaction is unavoidable but contained

This turns misreading into a choice rather than an accident.

**Quote test**

Pull three sentences at random. Read them as if quoted by someone hostile.

If they misrepresent your intent, revise. Durable writing doesn't collapse when taken out of context.

### Additional Checks

These are not rubrics. They don't need to "pass." They're questions that catch failure modes the rubrics miss.

**Novelty check**

If I strip away the framing, what is the single new distinction, lens, or constraint this introduces?

If I can't name one, the piece may be eloquent but empty.

**Friction check**

After reading this, does the reader have a clearer mental model, even if no conclusion is given?

Openness is productive when it creates traction. Indefinite openness exhausts.

**Positioning check**

From whose position am I speaking? Who might read this as dismissive, even if that's not the intent?

This is not about tone-policing. It's about awareness of who the piece talks past or over.

**Definition check**

Identify the 1–3 most loaded terms in the piece. Either define them in-line, or replace them with something operational.

Words like "structure," "signal," "alignment," "reasoning" often masquerade as shared understanding when they aren't. If you can't define them, the post is probably operating at slogan level.

**Causality check**

For each major conclusion, write a one-sentence "because."

If the "because" is hand-wavy, either supply the missing link or downgrade the claim. Many posts feel generated because they skip the middle of the reasoning: observation → conclusion without the connecting step.

**Concrete anchor**

Include at least one of: a real example, a specific failure mode, a constraint, a measurable quantity, a named situation.

A metaphor is not an anchor. If the piece is purely conceptual, the anchor can be a worked scenario (one paragraph) rather than evidence.

**Evidence toggle** (for empirical claims)

If you're making a claim about the world (numbers, trends, "research shows"), either:
- cite it, or
- reframe as hypothesis, or
- give a concrete anecdote and label it as such

This is optional for exploratory posts, mandatory for anything claiming factual grounding.

**Scope check**

What is the claim about: mechanisms, incentives, costs, or outcomes?

Is it universal, typical, or conditional?

What domain does it apply to?

If the claim is broad, narrow it or name the boundary. This is the difference between "insight" and "hot take."

**Counterexample check**

Name one situation where the claim fails.

Either incorporate it (tighten scope) or explain why it's outside scope.

This prevents brittle generalizations and makes posts feel more honest without hedging.

**Skim test**

Read only headers + first sentence of each paragraph.

If the piece becomes misleading when skimmed, rewrite those first sentences.

This catches a real failure mode: accurate when read carefully, wrong when skimmed.

**Compression check**

Highlight the sentences that introduce new concepts.

If more than half the sentences introduce new concepts, the reader will drown. Insert a sentence that cashes out implications or grounds the concept.

## The Process

### Step 0: Capture

Write without an audience. Notes, fragments, voice memos, half-formed thoughts, irritation, private reasoning.

AI generates from nothing. Humans harvest. The difference is visible in the result.

Don't generate prose from a blank slate. Edit captured thinking into shape.

Example of capture to edit:

*Raw note:* "models just compress. compression = what's surprising. use that. expensive thing downstream needs less input. model is the filter not the thing"

*Edited:* "A model compresses its training data. When new input doesn't fit the compression, that's a novelty signal. If the downstream structure is expensive to update, use the model to filter what goes in."

The raw note is ugly. The edit is cleaner. But the edit came from somewhere real.

### Step 1: Draft

Write the piece. Don't worry about the rubrics yet. Get the ideas down.

### Step 2: Evaluate Rubric #1

Go through each story beat. Note which ones fail and why.

### Step 3: Evaluate Rubric #2

Hunt for AI tells. List every instance.

### Step 4: Evaluate Rubric #3

Read for flow. Mark every transition that's missing or abrupt.

### Step 5: Evaluate Rubric #4

Test adversarial robustness. Write the intent statements. Identify the strongest objection. Run the worst-case reader test. Pull quotes and check if they survive isolation.

### Step 6: Revise

Fix all issues found. Don't just patch—consider whether fixes create new problems.

### Step 7: Re-evaluate

Run all four rubrics again. If any fail, go back to Step 6.

### Step 8: Final read

Read it as a reader, not a writer. Does it land?

## Tempo Guide

Tempo creates emphasis. Use it deliberately.

**Short sentences punch.** Use after explanation to land a point.

**One-word or two-word paragraphs stop the reader.** Use sparingly for maximum effect.

"Train a model. Extract structure. Throw the model away."

"Scary."

**Long sentences carry complexity.** Use when multiple ideas need to connect.

**Variation is the point.** If three paragraphs in a row have the same rhythm, you have a problem.

---

## Common Failure Modes

**Too compressed** - Ideas need room to breathe. If every sentence is a new concept, the reader can't absorb.

**Too abstract** - Ground ideas before you abstract them. Concrete first, then pattern.

**Format showing** - If you can see the template, rewrite until you can't.

**Solving instead of framing** - Sometimes the piece is about a question, not an answer. Don't force a solution.

**Metronomic cadence** - Same length sentences, same length paragraphs, same rhythm throughout. This is the most common AI tell. Break it.

**Premature closure** - Sentences or paragraphs that make an idea feel resolved when it isn't. Symptoms include neat summaries, balanced contrasts, clean takeaways.

Ask: Does this paragraph close the idea or keep it open? If it closes it, be sure that's intentional.

---

## Tempo as the Unifying Principle

Most of the rules in this guide are downstream of one thing: tempo.

Triads are banned because they normalize tempo into three equal beats. Contrast pairs become tells when clustered because they create call-response rhythms that repeat. Parallel clauses impose metronomic structure. Staccato closers punch, but three punches in a row becomes a pattern. Emdashes break sentences in predictable ways.

The problem is never the structure itself. The problem is when structures create:

- **Normalized distribution**: equal spacing, equal weight, equal length
- **Tempo clusters**: multiple rhythm-heavy structures in the same area
- **Predictable beats**: the reader can feel what's coming next

Flow is tempo at the paragraph and section level. A piece with good micro-tempo (varied sentences) can still fail if every section has the same arc or every transition uses the same move.

### When to break the rules

A rule exists to prevent a failure mode. If you understand the failure mode, you can break the rule when it doesn't apply.

**Triads**: banned because they normalize tempo and signal closure. But if the content genuinely has three parts and you need the closure signal, a triad might be correct. The test: would two items or four items be dishonest? If three is the true number, consider keeping it—but vary the length of each item so the tempo isn't perfectly normalized.

**Contrast pairs**: rationed because they cluster tempo. But a hook lives or dies on impact. If a contrast pair is the strongest version of your hook, use it there and budget elsewhere.

**Parallel clauses**: banned because they impose metronomic structure. But parallel structure can create intentional emphasis when the repetition *is* the point. The test: does the parallelism serve the idea or just sound nice? If removing it weakens the meaning, keep it.

**Staccato closers**: rationed because they become tics. But some ideas need a hard stop. If the paragraph earns the punch, take it—just don't take it again for a while.

The meta-rule: every structure has a tempo cost. Spend deliberately. If you've spent heavily in one area, go light elsewhere. If you need to break a rule, understand what you're buying and what it costs.

## Example Transformation

Examples are illustrative, not templates. If you find yourself copying the shape of an example, you're probably reintroducing performance.

**AI-style:**
"There are three key benefits to this approach. First, it's more efficient. Second, it's more scalable. Third, it's more maintainable. These benefits combine to create a compelling case for adoption."

**Revised:**
"It's faster and scales better. Maintenance is where it actually matters though. When something breaks you can find the problem without rebuilding the whole system."

The revision:
- Breaks the triad into uneven groupings
- Prioritizes one point over the others (maintenance)
- Includes a concrete reason (finding the problem)
- Doesn't summarize itself

Note: this is still somewhat crafted. Real captured writing would be rougher. The goal isn't to imitate this shape—it's to see what's being removed.

## Voice vs. Tone

Tone is how you sound in a given moment. Voice is how you sound across everything you write.

Tone shifts with context: serious for a difficult topic, lighter for an aside. Voice stays constant. It's the set of choices you make so consistently that readers recognize you without a byline.

Voice emerges from:
- **Sentence rhythm preferences**: do you tend short or long? Do you vary by feel or by rule?
- **Vocabulary range**: technical or plain? Latinate or Anglo-Saxon?
- **Distance from reader**: intimate ("you and I both know") or removed ("one might observe")?
- **Relationship to uncertainty**: do you hedge, assert, or question?
- **What you find funny**: this leaks through even in serious writing

You can't construct voice deliberately. You can only notice what you already do and protect it during editing. The danger is revising voice out of a piece in pursuit of correctness.

Test: read your draft aloud. Where do you stumble? Sometimes that's bad writing. Sometimes that's your voice fighting the edit.

## Compression vs. Expansion

Density is a choice, not a virtue.

**Compress when:**
- The reader already has context
- The point is simple and doesn't need elaboration
- You're building momentum toward something
- Expansion would dilute impact

**Expand when:**
- The idea is unfamiliar and needs grounding
- You're asking the reader to change their mind
- The logic has steps that can't be skipped
- Rushing would create misunderstanding

The failure mode in both directions: compression that loses the reader, expansion that loses their attention.

A piece can alternate. Compress through familiar territory, expand at the crux, compress again for the landing. The rhythm of compression and expansion is itself a tempo choice.

Test: for each paragraph, ask whether a reader could skip it and still follow the piece. If yes, either the paragraph is earning its place through something other than information (rhythm, grounding, voice) or it should be cut.

## Abstraction Laddering

Every piece moves between concrete and abstract. The question is when and how.

**Ground floor**: specific examples, named situations, particular details
**Middle floors**: patterns, categories, "things like this"
**Top floor**: principles, theories, universal claims

The guide says "ground before abstracting." That's the minimum. Better pieces move up and down throughout.

**Ladder up** when: you've given enough concrete detail that the pattern is almost obvious, and naming it crystallizes
**Ladder down** when: you've made a claim abstract enough that the reader needs to see it working in practice

Staying too high reads as hand-wavy. Staying too low reads as meandering. The movement itself creates the sense that the piece is going somewhere.

Common failure: laddering up at the end of every section. "And that's why X matters." This becomes a tic. Sometimes end concrete. Let the reader climb the ladder themselves.

## Distribution Patterns

Irregular distribution solves the first-order problem: don't cluster, don't normalize.

But you can have irregular distributions that repeat the same *shape* of irregularity. If every time you use a rationed pattern you place it one-third of the way through a section, that's a distribution pattern. If you always put your punch at the end of paragraph two, that's a distribution pattern.

The goal is avoiding repetition at every order:
- **First order**: don't repeat the pattern itself too often
- **Second order**: don't repeat the spacing between instances
- **Third order**: don't repeat the distribution shape across sections

This is hard to see in your own writing. One trick: map where your rationed patterns land across the whole piece. Look at the map. If it has a rhythm, break it.

Another trick: compare distribution across multiple pieces you've written. Habits hide at the level of the corpus, not the individual piece. If you always put contrast pairs in hooks, you have a tell.

## Audience Calibration

The guide assumes a general intelligent reader. Real pieces have specific audiences.

**Technical vs. general**: Technical audiences tolerate compression and jargon; general audiences need expansion and translation. But technical audiences also notice when you're padding, and general audiences notice condescension. Match density to what they already know, not to your assumptions about their intelligence.

**Sympathetic vs. hostile**: Sympathetic readers give you the benefit of the doubt on ambiguous sentences. Hostile readers take the worst interpretation. For hostile audiences, every sentence needs to be defensible in isolation. For sympathetic audiences, you can rely more on context and tone.

**Peers vs. public**: Peers read for the idea. Public reads for the takeaway. Peers tolerate "I don't know yet." Public often wants resolution. You can resist this—some of the best public writing preserves genuine uncertainty—but know you're doing it.

Calibration doesn't mean pandering. It means knowing what work the reader will do and what work you need to do for them.

## Knowing When a Piece Is Done

A piece is done when further revision trades off against itself.

Early revisions fix problems. Late revisions fix problems that earlier revisions created. At some point, you're pushing the same clay around: fixing one thing breaks another, and fixing that breaks the first thing again.

Signs you're done:
- Revisions are getting smaller and more local
- You're reverting changes you made two passes ago
- The piece sounds like you and holds together when you read it cold
- You're editing for preference, not for improvement

Signs you're not done:
- You can feel something wrong but can't name it
- Reading it aloud makes you wince at specific moments
- The rubrics fail
- You're avoiding a section because fixing it is hard

Overworking kills voice. Underworking leaves holes. The goal is to stop at the point where the piece is *yours* and *solid*, even if not perfect. Perfection is a form of voice loss.

## Idea Selection

Not every thought is ready to be a piece.

**Ready:**
- You have a genuine position, not just a topic
- You can state the hypothesis in one sentence
- You know who would disagree and why they're wrong (or where you're uncertain)
- You've been thinking about it long enough that you've surprised yourself at least once

**Not ready:**
- You have a topic but no angle
- Your hypothesis is "X is interesting" or "X is important"
- You don't know what the counterargument is
- You're excited but haven't done the thinking yet

Writing can be a way to develop thinking, but publishing is not. Draft to figure out what you believe. Publish when you know.

Test: can you explain your piece's argument in conversation without notes? If you fumble, you don't know what you think yet. Write more. Publish later.

---

## Final Check

Before publishing, ask:

1. Does the hook create tension?
2. Is the problem felt, not just stated?
3. Does every section earn its place?
4. Are the transitions invisible?
5. Does the ending resonate with the opening?
6. Would a reader know this was AI-written?
7. What would a hostile reader accuse this of saying?
8. Do isolated quotes misrepresent the intent?

If any answer is wrong, revise.
