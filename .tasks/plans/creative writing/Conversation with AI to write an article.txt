The Two Failures That Define the Ceiling in RAG
nestharus
Jan 07, 2026
View stats in the app

I’ve been building a retrieval system that treats RAG differently than the standard approach. The architecture came from a strange place: I was playing with sorting algorithms and noticed something about alignment that I couldn’t unsee. That observation eventually became a spec for what I’m calling Gen 3 RAG.

This post walks through the journey, the failures I was trying to solve, and how the system actually works.
Where the intuition came from

I started with a number line.

Direction only exists after you pick a basis. That insight came from playing with Bubble Sort, watching it swap adjacent out-of-order items until the list becomes a clean ramp. Bubble Sort taught me alignment exists: aligned means directions agree with the basis, misaligned means they fight it, sorting is paying energy to reduce misalignment.

Then I broke Selection Sort by pulling from the center instead of the end. Start with 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Pull from the middle and land at 3, 2, 4, 1, 5. The direction pattern alternates: down, up, down, up. Relative to the number line basis, misaligned. Relative to the alternating pattern, perfectly aligned.

That was when I stopped chasing “flat line” as the only target. The target could be a wave shape. Two directions interleaved makes a wave. Five vectors alternating makes a wave with more structure.

Then I asked the question that breaks the clean picture: what if the vectors blink in and out? What if direction is probabilistic? Now you have overlapping waves present with different probabilities. That needed a container concept: many waves, uncertainty, superposition-like overlap, residue that fits nowhere cleanly. I call that container a cloud.

Vectors encode direction. Waves encode vectors. Clouds encode waves.

This became my lens for looking at RAG. The retrieval systems we build today have a ceiling, and I think I can finally name what creates it.
The ceiling

Standard RAG makes a quiet bet: that vector similarity is truth. You chunk your documents, embed the chunks, store them in a vector database. At query time, you embed the query and retrieve whatever lands closest.

Two assumptions hold this together. First, the embedding is correct: whatever the embedder says about where a chunk belongs, that’s ground truth. Second, chunks are independent: proximity in the document, relationships to other concepts, contradictions with other sources, none of that survives the embedding step.

Gen 2 systems like GraphRAG added structure on top. Entity extraction, knowledge graphs, summarization passes, hierarchies of abstraction. This helps. You get better global queries because you retrieve summaries that capture themes. The structure gives you something to traverse.

Gen 2 still inherits both assumptions. The graph is built on top of the vector space, as an addition rather than a correction. If the embedder puts two unrelated concepts near each other, the graph faithfully connects them. If two documents contradict each other, the summaries average them into mush.

I kept running into two failure modes that this architecture handles poorly.
Failure mode 1: the embedder conflates what should be separate

Your embedding model was trained on a general corpus. Your documents are specific. The embedder learned that certain words and phrases cluster together because they co-occurred in the training data. Your domain has distinctions the training data had no reason to make.

A note on literal differences: some conflation happens because single-vector embeddings pool away token-level detail. ColBERT and other late-interaction models address this by keeping all token embeddings and computing MaxSim at retrieval time. If your problem is “port 8080 vs port 8090,” late interaction handles it.

The harder problem is semantic conflation at the concept level. Say your corpus has “compliance audit” meaning regulatory review in some documents and code conformance check in others. Same words, different domains. Late interaction keeps all the tokens, but the token embeddings themselves are identical in both contexts. The embedder learned one meaning; your organization uses two.
Failure mode 2: the corpus contradicts itself

Documents disagree. Policies get updated. Sources have different perspectives. This is normal.

Vector retrieval has one representation for disagreement: proximity. When you retrieve chunks that contradict each other, they just show up in the context window together. The LLM has to figure out that they conflict, decide which to trust, and synthesize an answer. Sometimes it does this well. Often it averages.

This gets worse over time. Old information sits next to new information. Conflicting sources coexist peacefully in the index because the index treats them as equally valid. Your retrieval quality degrades, and the only signal is that answers get less reliable.
What I built

The system has two interlocking parts. One handles meaning: how it flows through the corpus, where it conflicts, when to hold competing interpretations. The other handles perception: detecting when the embedding space is failing and patching it locally. They feed each other.

Let me walk through how it works by following a piece of text through the system.
Text arrives

Picture text flowing in word by word. Each token gets embedded as it arrives. A boundary detector watches the embedding shift between consecutive tokens: the cosine distance between b_t and b_{t-1}. It also watches punctuation, structure markers like headers and code blocks, entity changes.

This is Bayesian online changepoint detection. The system maintains a running probability that the semantic direction just changed. When that probability crosses a threshold, the accumulated tokens become a span. The span becomes a node in the graph.

This produces variable-length chunks that respect the natural structure of the content. Dense technical prose might produce short spans, lots of direction changes. Flowing narrative might produce longer spans, stable direction. The system discovers the units in your content rather than imposing arbitrary token windows.

The node lands in a hot working area where active processing happens. It arrives with high uncertainty, knowing only what the embedder said about where it belongs.
Finding a home

The new evidence node needs to attach to an idea. Ideas are abstract handles that collect related evidence over time. They live in the colder tiers of the system, where they’ve accumulated structure through previous evidence.

The system searches for candidate ideas using the evidence node’s embedding. Candidates come back and face two gates. The first gate is fast: cosine similarity, structural overlap, basic compatibility. This is cheap, runs on everything. Most candidates fail here.

The ones that pass face the second gate: validation. Maybe an LLM call, maybe a classifier, maybe domain-specific rules. Validation is expensive, so it only runs on candidates that passed the fast gate. This is the core pattern: geometry proposes, validation commits.

If a candidate passes both gates, the evidence attaches to it via a membership edge. That idea just got stronger, more anchored, more likely to stay activated.

If nothing passes, a new idea sprouts. The system creates an abstract handle, seeds it with the evidence’s embedding, and places it in a cooler tier. This idea is provisional, uncertain, alone. It will either attract more evidence and thrive, or starve and sink toward cold storage.
Structure builds automatically

Beyond the membership edge to its idea, the evidence node gets structural edges. Adjacency edges connect it to the previous span, preserving sequential flow. Containment edges connect it to whatever paragraph, section, or document it belongs to, preserving hierarchy. Reference edges connect it to anything it explicitly mentions.

The graph grows as text flows in. Nodes at different levels (sentence, paragraph, section, document, idea) connect through typed edges. The structure emerges from the content itself.
The field relaxes

With the evidence placed and edges built, the field solver wakes up. It looks at the neighborhood around the new node and runs relaxation.

Here’s the key idea: each node has two embeddings. The base embedding is what the embedder originally said. The field embedding is where the node actually sits after accounting for structure. The field solver finds equilibrium between anchoring (stay near your base observation) and smoothness (stay near your neighbors).

Relaxation only runs locally, on the neighborhood around what just changed. The radius depends on how hot the node is. Focus nodes get wider radius, more relaxation steps. Context nodes get tighter updates. This keeps compute bounded while letting influence propagate.

After relaxation, the field embeddings in that neighborhood have shifted. The new evidence is incorporated. If it agrees with its neighbors, they pull together. If it disagrees, tension increases.
Tension surfaces conflict

The system tracks tension: the energy from edges where connected nodes have very different field embeddings. High tension means the constraints are fighting. Something is asking two nodes to be similar that are refusing to be similar.

A naive approach would let large disagreements dominate. One weird edge could distort a whole neighborhood. Instead, the system uses a robust loss that caps the influence of outliers. Small disagreements behave normally. Large disagreements transition to linear influence, preventing them from overwhelming everything else.

This turns outliers into diagnostics rather than distortions. Instead of silently warping the field, a large disagreement gets flagged: these two nodes are structurally connected, they should be similar, but something is forcing them apart. That’s worth investigating.

Some tension is noise. But persistent tension is signal. The system maintains a queue of conflicts ranked by score, which combines tension, uncertainty, and how often that region gets accessed. A background process pops the highest-priority conflict and investigates.

Investigation means fetching the relevant spans and running validation. If validation supports the edge, the system increases the gate, letting the edge influence smoothing more strongly. If validation contradicts the edge, the gate decreases, letting the nodes diverge. If the conflict persists after multiple attempts, the system branches.
Branching preserves ambiguity

When conflict persists, the system forks. Instead of one field state, there are now two: one hypothesis where node A’s interpretation dominates, another where node B’s dominates.

The branching is local. Only the contested nodes and their immediate neighborhood get forked. The rest of the graph stays shared. This keeps memory bounded. You can have multiple hypotheses about a disputed region while keeping the rest of the corpus unified.

Each hypothesis has its own field embeddings for the contested nodes. Each has its own gate values on the contested edges. Queries can specify which hypothesis to use, or surface the ambiguity explicitly.

This is what makes contradiction queryable. The system can tell you “these two interpretations exist, here’s the evidence for each, here’s where they disagree.” Hypotheses can resolve later when new evidence tips the balance. They can also persist indefinitely if the corpus genuinely contains unresolved disagreement. Reality is sometimes ambiguous. The system represents that instead of hiding it.
Tiers breathe

Not everything stays hot. Nodes that keep receiving evidence or getting retrieved stay activated. Nodes that go quiet decay. The system promotes and demotes based on activation scores.

Focus is the hot scratchpad: dozens of nodes actively being processed. Active is recently used: hundreds to thousands. Context is relevant background: tens of thousands. Inactive is cold storage: everything else, on disk.

Tier caps enforce hard limits. If Focus is full and a new node needs to enter, something else demotes. This bounds RAM and compute regardless of corpus size. A billion nodes in Inactive, bounded latency on the hot path.
The sleep cycle

Local relaxation accumulates drift. Each update is approximately right, but approximations compound. The field can develop global inconsistencies.

Periodically, the system runs a sleep cycle. Think of it as closing its eyes to digest what it learned during the day.

While ingestion continues in the foreground, a background job freezes a snapshot at a specific moment in the event log. This snapshot sees everything up to that moment and nothing after. The world keeps moving; the snapshot stays frozen.

On that frozen snapshot, the system runs a full global field solve. Not local relaxation but the robust solver working across everything, iteratively reweighting edges based on disagreement until it converges to equilibrium.

When the solve finishes, the system catches up. Events happened during consolidation. It reads them from the log, applies them to the new state, does local repair around the touched nodes. The new state absorbs everything that happened while it was thinking.

Then it publishes. An atomic pointer swap: readers who started before the swap finish on the old view, new readers see the healed state. A grace period ensures nobody gets cut off mid-read.

Ingestion keeps running. Reads keep running. The system self-heals in the background while everything else continues.
Meanwhile, in perception

Everything so far handles meaning: how it flows, where it conflicts, how to represent ambiguity. But what if the embedding space itself is systematically wrong?

The semantic layer can surface tension and conflict. Some of that is real: the documents genuinely disagree. Some of it is artifactual: the embedder conflated concepts that should be separate, creating false similarity that the field solver then struggles with.

The system needs a way to tell the difference and fix the perception problem.
Strain accumulates

Every retrieval event produces two rankings. The embedding ranking comes from vector similarity. The reasoning ranking comes from LLM or validator judgment about what’s actually relevant.

When these disagree, that’s strain. The reasoning layer is saying “document A should rank above document B” while the embeddings say the opposite. One disagreement is noise. The LLM might be uncertain, the query might be ambiguous, the judgment might be borderline. The system waits for patterns.

But if the same region of embedding space keeps producing the same kind of failure, that’s a pattern. Strain events get clustered by query region. Each cluster maintains a decayed accumulator: recent strain counts more than old strain.

A cluster triggers only when three conditions are met. Enough events: you need volume to distinguish signal from noise. Sustained strain: the accumulator stays above threshold over time. Consistent judgments: the reasoning layer keeps agreeing with itself about what’s wrong. If the LLM contradicts itself across events, the signal is too noisy to act on.

When all three conditions hold, the cluster triggers and lens training begins.
Lenses correct locally

The fix is to patch locally, leaving the base embedder frozen.

The system maintains a frozen base embedder plus small correction lenses. Each lens is a low-rank residual that activates in a specific region of embedding space. Low-rank means it has limited capacity: enough to make targeted corrections, constrained enough to stay local.

Retrieval happens in two stages. First, standard ANN search over the base embeddings to get candidates. This is fast and uses your existing indices. Second, the lenses activate on the query and candidates, apply their corrections, and rerank.

This means you can fix embedding failures while keeping your indices intact. The base vectors stay stable. All your existing infrastructure still works. The lenses apply at query time, adjusting only what needs adjusting. You can deploy a fix in minutes instead of reprocessing your corpus.

Training data comes from the accumulated strain events: triplets of (query, should-rank-higher, should-rank-lower). The lens learns the minimum correction needed to separate what needs separating. A regularizer penalizes large corrections, keeping the lens focused on its region.
When lenses need help

Sometimes a low-rank correction lacks the capacity to capture what’s missing. The system watches for signs: training loss plateauing, strain persisting after deployment.

When this happens, it escalates. First, increase the lens capacity. If that helps, done. If that’s still insufficient, add a second lens for the same region. If that still falls short, flag a foundation gap: the base embedder is missing something that requires deeper changes.

This gives a principled stopping point. You escalate until strain decreases. If you exhaust the options, you know the problem is deeper than retrieval correction.
The loop closes

Here’s how the two parts talk to each other.

The semantic layer surfaces where meaning is unstable: high tension, conflicts that persist, regions where the field solver struggles. Some of that instability comes from the content. Some comes from perception drift.

The geometric layer’s strain mechanism distinguishes them. If a high-tension region also produces retrieval failures that the reasoning layer keeps correcting, that’s evidence the embedder is wrong. Train a lens, patch the space.

If you patch the space and tension remains, the conflict is semantic. The documents genuinely disagree. Hold the fork.

Perception feeds reasoning. Reasoning audits perception. They converge toward a representation that is both structurally coherent and geometrically sound.
What this looks like in practice

At ingestion: text streams in, boundary detection finds natural chunks, evidence nodes land in Focus, ideas sprout or get reinforced in Context, structural edges build, local field relaxation runs, the graph grows and the field shifts.

At query time: retrieval uses the field embeddings (structure-aware, noise-smoothed) with lens corrections applied. If the query hits a region with unresolved hypotheses, the system can surface the ambiguity or pick based on policy.

In the background: strain accumulates from retrieval events, clusters trigger lens training, the sleep cycle runs global consolidation, the conflict resolver works through the queue. The system improves on its own.
Why this matters

Production RAG systems degrade. You ship something that works on your test set, and six months later it gives weird answers. This happens because the failure modes are silent. The embedder’s conflations stay hidden. The corpus’s contradictions stay invisible. Everything looks fine until it drifts too far.

Gen 3 is about making failures visible and correctable. The semantic layer represents where meaning is contested. The geometric layer detects where perception is failing. Together they surface what’s going wrong and provide machinery to fix it.

The embeddings are observations. The corpus contains contradictions. Once you build for that reality, you can handle it.

I’m still working through edge cases. If you’re building retrieval systems that need to handle contradiction, ambiguity, or domain-specific embedding failures, I’d be curious to compare notes.
